## 1. Candidate Problem  
`20 pts`

> Two sentences. One problem from your own experience: your major, a job, a campus
> process, a family business, a team. Pick something you actually care about.

I run a college soccer recruiting business that helps high school athletes with the recruiting process, and one problem I have is turning interested athletes into paying clients. I can get athletes to respond and show interest in getting recruiting help, but getting from that initial interest to speaking with their parents and eventually becoming a paying client has been difficult.

## 2. Wickedness Test  
`25 pts`

> Evaluate your problem against at least FOUR characteristics from Session 1A.
> One short paragraph or four labeled bullets. End with an honest verdict.
>
> Available characteristics: hard to formulate a problem statement; no stopping rule;
> solutions are good or bad rather than true or false; no immediate or ultimate test;
> one-shot solutions with no trial and error; no exhaustively describable solution set;
> may be a symptom of another problem; the discrepancy has multiple valid explanations;
> the problem solver is liable for consequences.
>
> "This is actually a tame problem" is a valid verdict when the reasoning is sound.

I think this problem has several characteristics of a wicked problem. First, it is hard to formulate one clear problem statement because there are several ways I could explain what is going wrong. I could say that I am not getting enough clients, my sales process is not effective enough, parents are not involved early enough, or athletes do not see enough value to take the next step. There is also no real stopping rule because even if I improve the percentage of athletes who become clients, I could always improve the process further. The problem also has multiple valid explanations because an athlete might not become a client due to price, lack of urgency, their parents not seeing the value, choosing another recruiting service, or something I am doing wrong in my process. Finally, I am liable for the consequences of the decisions I make. For example, contacting parents earlier might help me get more clients, but it could also come across as too aggressive and cause some families to lose interest.

Verdict:I believe this is a wicked problem because there is no single explanation or correct solution. Different ways of framing the problem could lead me toward completely different solutions, and I have to make decisions without knowing for certain which approach will work best.

## 3. Three Framings  
`30 pts`

> Three DISTINCT problem statements for the same situation. The test: a reader should
> not be able to swap two of them without changing what would get built.

**Framing A (the obvious one):My business is not converting enough interested student athletes into paying clients. This perspectives shows the problem is mainly my sales process and figuring out how to get more interested athletes to take the next step and become clients.

**Framing B (stakeholder shift): From the parent's perspective, the problem could be that they do not have enough information or trust in my recruiting service to feel comfortable paying for it. An athlete might be interested in getting help, but if their parents do not understand what they are paying for or why it would help their child, the conversation may never go any further.

**Framing C (goal shift): The real goal is not just getting more athletes onto sales calls or convincing more people to pay. The problem could instead be creating a process where athletes and their families understand the value of getting recruiting help and feel that continuing to work with me is worth it.

---

## 4. Commit and Justify  
`included in Framings`

> Pick one framing. Defend it in a short paragraph. Then name what your choice makes
> invisible. Every framing forecloses something.

**Chosen framing:I would choose Framing C, the goal shift.

**Why: I think Framing C gets closer to what I actually want to accomplish with the business. I could focus completely on getting more people onto calls or writing better sales messages, but that does not necessarily solve why families are not moving forward in the first place. If athletes and parents already understand the value of what I provide before I ask them to become a client, I think the sales process would become much easier. It also gives me more possible ways to approach the problem instead of assuming that I just need to become better at selling.

**What this framing makes invisible: This framing could make problems with my actual sales process less visible. Even if families understand the value of the service, I still have to clearly explain the price, answer their questions, follow up at the right time, and actually ask them to become a client. If I focus too much on providing value before the sale, I could overlook problems that are happening later in the process.

## 5. Oath Check  
`15 pts`

> You already assumed a tool. Everyone does. Name it honestly, then state what would
> have to be true about your problem for that tool to be the justified choice.

**The tool I already assumed: The tool I have already assumed is social media direct messaging, especially using platforms like X and Instagram to contact student-athletes.

**What would have to be true for it to be justified: Social media DMs would be justified if the athletes I am trying to reach are actually active on those platforms, consistently see and respond to my messages, and those conversations eventually lead to serious recruiting conversations and clients. If another method like email, referrals, content, or reaching parents directly produces better results, then I should be willing to use that instead of assuming DMs are the best choice just because they are what I already use.

## 6. Adequacy Sketch  
`10 pts`

> Two sentences. What would "good enough for now" look like, and how would you know
> you had reached it? (Worth 10 points. Do not skip this one.)

A good enough outcome for now would be having a process where a consistent percentage of interested athletes move from the first conversation into a serious conversation involving their parents and eventually become clients. I would know I had reached that point by tracking how many athletes respond, how many move forward to serious conversations or calls, and how many of those families eventually become paying clients.
